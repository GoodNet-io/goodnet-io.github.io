No One Took Your Freedom

GoodNet-io — May 2026

Every few years someone very rich stands on a stage and offers to give you freedom.

This time it arrives as an app. One place for everything: your messages, your money, your friends, your identity. Encrypted, they say. Private, they say. Frictionless. You will never have to leave. And that last part is true in a way they do not mean.

It is worth slowing down on the offer itself, because the offer is the trick. Not the product — the offer. The moment someone whose business is keeping you inside promises to set you free, you are looking at a contradiction wearing a smile. A company cannot sell you freedom for the same reason a cage cannot sell you the sky. Its entire value is the wall. If leaving cost you nothing, there would be no business. So the better the cage, the more it must cost to walk out — and they will call that comfort.

But I do not want to write about them. They are a symptom. I want to write about the thing underneath, because once you see it you cannot unsee it, and it is much older than any app.

The pattern is always the same

Here is how a free thing becomes a toll booth. It has happened so many times that it reads like a law of physics.

First, something new exists and it is free — not because anyone is generous, but because nobody wants it yet. Internet addresses were handed out for the asking in the 1980s; whole universities got blocks larger than the entire internet of some continents today. It cost nothing because it was worth nothing.

Then everybody wants it. The free thing becomes scarce — sometimes naturally, often on purpose. The addresses ran out. A secondary market appeared. The thing that was given away for a signature now sells for real money, and a resource that was once a common becomes a line on someone's balance sheet.

Then the scarcity becomes rent: a stream of payments you make not for anything produced, but for the simple fact that someone now stands between you and the thing you need. This happened to addresses. It happened to computing, where the traffic into the cloud is free and the traffic out is a penalty — a fee for the crime of leaving. It happened to video formats, where for years you could not write a program that played a movie without paying a patent pool that often did not build anything at all. It happened to open source, where licenses that began as gifts mutated, under investor pressure, into permission slips.

The same four steps, every time. Something common. Then scarce. Then owned. Then rented. The faces change; the shape does not.

And now it is reaching the last two things that were still, somehow, yours: the act of talking to another person, and the act of paying them. That is what the everything-app is. Not an innovation. The next floor of the same building.

What you were actually promised

The strange part is that the original thing was not a gift from a company at all. It was an architecture, and the architecture had freedom built into its bones.

The people who designed the internet wrote the principle down plainly: keep the middle stupid and the edges smart. The network in between should do one dumb job — move bytes — and ask no permission. Everyone who connects is a peer. No privileged center, no toll booth in the middle by design. From that one decision came three promises, and the nineties stated them out loud.

Distance would stop costing money. A letter to the other side of the planet would arrive in milliseconds and cost the same as a letter next door. For the first time in history, distance was no longer rent.

Time would belong to you. The whole point of a message instead of a call was that you answer when you can. No one interrupts you. No one blocks your attention. The conversation waits for you, not the other way around.

And anyone could speak. Get an address, and you can publish. Know how to type a link, and you can read. The old asymmetry — a few broadcast, the many receive — was, at the level of the wires, simply gone.

These were not dreams. They were written into the protocols themselves. Products grew out of them. You grew up inside them.

What happened to the three

Look at where each promise stands now, because the scoreboard tells the whole story.

The first one held. Distance is still nearly free; a page from a server in another country opens in a fraction of a second. Good. Keep it.

The second one was inverted. The protocols that let you answer when you wanted are still there — but the culture that made them humane was quietly killed by two small engineering choices: the presence indicator and the push notification. Add those, and a message becomes a call. It demands you now, because if you do not answer, a second one comes, then a third. A company that sells the tools measured its own users and found the average worker interrupted every two minutes, opening mail before six in the morning, sitting in meetings that no one scheduled. The asynchronous world was built and then buried. Not by accident — by defaults that could have been chosen differently.

The third one was sold. Your behavior, once a byproduct, turned out to be sellable to advertisers at margins no normal business has ever seen, and so "free" services were built on top of watching you. The square where anyone could speak became a handful of private rooms, each owned, each watching, each deciding what you may say and whether you may leave with your friends when you go.

So when the next person on the next stage offers to give you freedom, hold that scoreboard up against the offer. Two of three promises were taken not by force but by default — by a thousand small choices made at the level of apps and protocols, each of which could have gone the other way. Nobody kicked down your door. The walls went up one convenient feature at a time, and most of them were marketed as gifts.

No one took it

Here is the part they need you not to notice.

Your freedom was never taken. It cannot be taken, because it was never theirs to hold. It is not a permission someone grants and someone else revokes. It is a property of how things are arranged — and arrangements can be changed, by anyone, at any time, because the people who built the first ones were not gods. They were engineers making choices. Every wall you live behind today is a choice someone made and called permanent. None of them is permanent. They are held up, every single day, only by everyone agreeing to stand inside them.

The presence indicator can be off. The notification can wait. The identity can be a key you carry instead of an account someone lends you. The connection between two people can be exactly as direct as those two people decide it should be — with no one in the middle unless they choose to put someone there. None of this requires permission. It requires only that enough people remember it was always possible, and stop mistaking the cage for the weather.

That is the whole secret, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. The systems that have outlived every walled garden — the ones that quietly became the ground everything else stands on — never tried to be your world. They refused to be the center. They handed you the pieces and got out of the way, and they survived precisely because they trusted that people, given the pieces, want to build something better and will. They run on that trust. They have always run on that trust. It is the only fuel that has never run out.

No one is going to hand you your freedom from a stage, because no one has it to hand. You have it now. You have it every day. The only walls around you are the ones you still agree to. And the moment enough of us stop agreeing, the toll booths in the middle become what they always secretly were — optional.

And it is not a thing to wait for. The pieces exist now. There is a core — open, under a license no one can take back — that does one honest job: it lets two people reach each other as directly as they choose, with nothing in the middle they did not put there. It is years of work, and it carries a quiet discipline: every claim it makes about itself comes with the way to check it. You are not asked to believe anything.

It is not a world, and it refuses to be one. It is a canvas with the hard parts already solved — the parts that take years and turn most people back — on which anyone can draw a way of connecting that does not yet exist: a messenger, a market, a private commons, whatever a person needs and cannot find because no company found it worth building. The day real things grow on top of it, it moves on its own — without permission, and in time without me. That is not a worry; it is the design. A thing that needs its author is fragile. A thing built to outlive him was the point.

But that is the smaller story, for another day. The larger one is the one you can act on without me, or anyone:

Nobody took your freedom. Stop waiting to be given it back.
